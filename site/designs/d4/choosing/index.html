Choosing for customers

When you register a domain for someone else, the main decision is who the registrant is. The rest follows from it.

Our reading, not a sourced fact. This page is our advice for agencies and developers. It is not a quoted source, except where a source is cited.

Who should own the domain

Make the customer the registrant, with their own name or organisation and a contact address they control. You act as the technical or administrative contact, or hold a delegated login. The reasons are in the sources:

  • The registrant is the party the transfer rules treat as holder. A change of registrant triggers a confirmation process and, by default, a 60-day transfer lock, so putting the right name on the registration at the start avoids the lock later. Source: ICANN, Transfer Policy. Checked 25 Nov 2026.
  • The auth code identifies the holder, and the registrar must provide it within five calendar days or remove the transfer lock. A customer who is the registrant can move away from you. Source: ICANN, Transfer Policy. Checked 25 Nov 2026.
  • The UK NCSC advises an organisation-controlled registrar account secured with good account practice, because attackers may try to hijack a domain. Source: UK NCSC, protecting your public domain name. Checked 25 Nov 2026.

Access

  • Prefer a registrar that lets the customer keep an account and grant you access, or an API key scoped to your work. Compare the "account security" and "bulk and reseller" columns on the registrars page.
  • Where you hold the account yourself, keep one account per customer or a clear record of which names belong to whom, and make sure the customer can obtain an auth code within the five-day rule.
  • Use two-factor authentication on every registrar account and turn the registrar lock on. Which registrars offer these is on the comparison, each with its source.

Records to keep

  • Domain, registrar, account used, registration and expiry dates, and where the auth code is stored (never in plain email).
  • Registrant name, organisation and the email on the registration. ICANN says registrars must publish a registrant email, often a web form or pseudonymised address, so check which is shown. Source: ICANN, advisory on registrars' contact web forms, May 2026 Not read on the official page: from a search summary. Checked 25 Nov 2026.
  • Nameservers, DNSSEC state and DS records if you enable it. Source: ICANN, DNSSEC: what it is and why it matters. Checked 25 Nov 2026.
  • Renewal arrangements: who pays, whether auto-renew is on, and which email receives reminders. ICANN requires reminders about a month and a week before expiry. Source: ICANN, Expired Registration Recovery Policy. Checked 25 Nov 2026.
  • The customer's written agreement that they are the owner.

Checklist: registering a domain on someone's behalf

  1. Check the name is free (registrar search or RDAP lookup); if it is taken, see the aftermarket.
  2. Confirm the registrar supports the extension you need (.uk, .eu and .pt vary: .eu eligibility conditions are noted at several registrars; read the notes on each entry).
  3. Read the first-year price against the renewal price and the transfer price, in the vendor's currency, and note whether tax is included.
  4. Enter the customer as registrant, with a role mailbox they control.
  5. Turn on two-factor authentication and the registrar lock.
  6. Turn on DNSSEC if your DNS provider and registrar support it together.
  7. Switch on auto-renew, or diarise the expiry and reminder emails.
  8. Record the details above and give the customer a copy.
  9. Note the transfer rules: 60 days after a change of registrant, and for .uk the tag change until 9 February 2027, then TACs. Source: ICANN, Transfer Policy; Nominet, .uk registry FAQ. Checked 25 Nov 2026.
